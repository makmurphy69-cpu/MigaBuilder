The plane can fly level wherever the thrust (orange) is above the drag (blue). Drag is high when slow (the wing needs a big angle to lift the weight) and when fast (air resistance). The gap between them is what you have for climbing. Solid lines at sea level, dashed at your cruise altitude.

How fast the plane climbs at full power at each altitude. The air gets thinner as you go up, so engines without a turbo lose power and the wing needs more speed. The service ceiling is where the climb falls to 30 m (100 ft) per minute.

The power (or thrust for a jet) your engines give at full throttle as you climb. A turbo-normalised piston engine keeps its sea-level power up high; a turboprop is flat-rated; an electric motor does not care about thin air, but the propeller still does.

A plane is stable in pitch when its centre of gravity (CG) is ahead of the neutral point (where the wing and tail together act). The distance between them is the static margin: 5–20% of the wing chord is comfortable. Too little and it is twitchy or unflyable; too much and it is nose-heavy and hard to flare for landing. A bigger tail moves the neutral point back.

Every weight in the plane. Wing loading (weight per square metre of wing) sets the stall speed; power loading sets the climb.

How well it flies

    How Plane Forge works

    Air: it uses the International Standard Atmosphere — the air is about a third thinner at 3,000 m than at sea level. Lift is ½·ρ·v²·S·CL; the wing stalls at its maximum lift coefficient, which flaps raise. Drag is the body drag (CD0) plus induced drag, which falls with a longer, slimmer wing (higher aspect ratio), plus wave drag near the speed of sound.

    Engines: piston power comes from the displacement, compression and revs, and falls with altitude unless it is turbo-normalised. A propeller turns power into thrust; its static thrust comes from momentum theory and its efficiency is about 80% in cruise. Jets give thrust directly, falling with thinner air and speed.

    Performance: the takeoff run is simulated step by step with rolling friction until the plane reaches lift-off speed, then climbs over a 15 m (50 ft) obstacle. Rate of climb is the extra power divided by the weight, worked out at every speed and altitude. Range keeps a 45-minute reserve. Stability uses the tail volume, wing aspect ratio and downwash to find the neutral point.

    These are estimates for learning and comparing designs, usually within 10–20% of real aircraft. A real aircraft needs a qualified designer, structural testing and approval from the aviation authority.

    About Plane Forge

    Plane Forge is an airplane design tool: start from a glider, microlight, trainer, bush plane, aerobatic plane, electric trainer, turboprop or business jet, change the wing, tail, engine and load, and see how well it would fly.

    How to use Plane Forge

    1. Pick a starting plane, then change the wingspan, wing area, flaps and tail.
    2. Choose the engine — piston, turboprop, jet or electric — and press “Take-off test”.
    3. Read the stall speed, takeoff distance, climb, ceiling, cruise, range and stability checks, look at the charts, and pin designs to compare them.

    Can I design a glider or an electric plane?

    Yes. Choose No engine for a glider to see its glide ratio and sink rate, or Electric for endurance and range from a battery.

    Is Plane Forge free?

    Yes. It is free to use, with no account, no watermark and no paywall.

    Are my files uploaded?

    No. It runs entirely in your browser, so your designs stay on your device.
